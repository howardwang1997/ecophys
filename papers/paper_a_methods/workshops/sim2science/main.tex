\title{Is Simulator Fidelity an Initialization Transient?\\
A Pre-Specified Audit of a Learned Market Model}
\author{Anonymous Author(s)}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Scientific simulators with persistent internal state are often evaluated from fresh, finite rollouts.
If initialization is not stationary, a fidelity score can mix startup relaxation with the regime it
purports to measure. We audit this failure mode in \method, a differentiable Langevin particle market
simulator whose return-tail score motivated a claim of stylized-fact fidelity. We pre-specify a
split-sample protocol: calibration trajectories set an energy-distance tolerance and the earliest
persistent plateau without using the Hill score or empirical target band; independent trajectories
are then scored on equal-length windows before and after that frozen point. Seven analytic conditions
(7,000 trajectories) yield 1/62
stationary false positives (Wilson 95\% upper bound 8.6\%) but only 28/124 cold-start detections,
showing high specificity and limited sensitivity. Across seven primary learned checkpoints,
\learnedScorable{} are scorable and \learnedConfirmed{} transfer the frozen gate; the paired Hill-index
effect is \learnedDelta{} (95\% interval \learnedCI{}). The finding is a simulator discrepancy, not
market physics, and the proposed audit is conservative rather than a universal stationarity test.
It converts an apparently favorable tail score into a falsifiable statement about when this simulator
is ready to evaluate.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Scientific simulators are imperfect: calibration can reduce parameter error while leaving structural
discrepancy between simulator and reality \citep{kennedy2001}. A more basic discrepancy can enter
\emph{before} calibration is judged. Stateful simulators are commonly launched from convenient
initial conditions, and finite-rollout statistics then mix an initial transient with the intended
long-run regime. Initialization bias is classical in discrete-event simulation
\citep{heidelberger1983,welch1983,white1997}, but it is easy to miss when a learned simulator is
evaluated by a nonlinear distributional summary rather than a sample mean.

We encountered this problem while evaluating \method, a differentiable market simulator with
persistent agent states. Financial return tails provide a sharp test: empirical returns are heavy
tailed \citep{cont2001,gabaix2003,plerou1999}, and a Hill estimate near the inverse-cubic regime is
usually counted as a successful stylized-fact match. In \method, however, the favorable estimate was
concentrated near initialization; later windows were lighter. Thus the same score supported two
opposite readings: realistic steady-state dynamics, or a startup artifact. Existing evidence on
time-varying apparent tails \citep{lebaron2001,warusawitharana2018,quintos2001} makes that distinction
scientifically material.

This paper makes three deliberately narrow contributions. First, it identifies and reproduces the
initialization-contamination failure in a learned Langevin simulator. Second, it pre-specifies a
split-sample audit that chooses a warm-up endpoint without using the Hill score or empirical target
band and preserves the scoring sample length. Third, it stress-tests that audit on analytic
stochastic processes, frozen learned checkpoints, and a within-family stochastic-volatility variant.
The outcome is an audit of an imperfect simulator: we neither infer a real-market mechanism from the
transient nor claim a universal convergence diagnostic.

\section{Simulator and failure mode}

\paragraph{Learned dynamics.}
\method{} evolves $N=10{,}000$ agents with latent states $s_i\in\mathbb{R}^{32}$ under overdamped
Langevin dynamics,
\begin{equation}
  s_{i,t+1}=s_{i,t}+\frac{\Delta t}{\gamma_i}
  \left(F^{\mathrm{cons}}_{i,\theta}+F^{\mathrm{diss}}_i\right)
  +\sqrt{\frac{2T_i\Delta t}{\gamma_i}}\,\epsilon_{i,t}+J_{i,t},
\end{equation}
where $F^{\mathrm{cons}}_{i,\theta}=-\nabla_{s_i}U_{\theta}$. The learned scalar potential combines an
external term with an unbiased random-pair estimate of a symmetric pair sum; $F^{\mathrm{diss}}$ is
quadratic drag. In the frozen configurations, $\epsilon$ is unit-variance Student-$t_5$, $J$ is a small
compound-Poisson displacement, and fixed agent types scale $T_i$ and $\gamma_i$. Aggregate state
motion forms excess demand, followed by linear or concave price impact and additive price noise. This
connects to Langevin market models \citep{bouchaud1998} and differentiable agent-based calibration
\citep{chopra2023,dyer2023}, but the present contribution is evaluation, not a first-of-kind model
claim. Models are trained against real-data stylized facts from two equity indices, gold, EUR/USD,
and BTC/USDT.

\paragraph{Contamination.}
Let $S(r_{0:T})$ be a fidelity statistic computed from a freshly initialized rollout. If the initial
law $q_0$ differs from the simulator's stationary or late-time law, $S$ estimates a mixture of
relaxation and late dynamics. Simply deleting $W$ samples creates a second confound when
$S(r_{W:T})$ uses fewer observations. We instead compare
\begin{equation}
 S_0=S(r_{0:L}),\qquad S_W=S(r_{W:W+L}),\quad L=4000,
 \label{eq:fixedlength}
\end{equation}
so that any change is not a sample-length artifact. Our main statistic is the Hill tail index
\citep{hill1975}; larger values indicate lighter tails.

\section{A frozen split-sample audit}

Figure~\ref{fig:protocol} summarizes the protocol. Every checkpoint produces 32 no-shock trajectories
with exactly $T=8000$ usable returns. Sixteen calibration trajectories determine robust scaling, a
tolerance, and $\wstar$; sixteen held-out trajectories are unopened until these quantities are
serialized and hashed.

\paragraph{Distributional state proxy.}
For each trajectory form delay vectors
$x_t=(r_t,|r_t|,|r_{t+1}|)$. Calibration-late observations ($t\in[6000,8000)$) set a componentwise
median and median absolute deviation; no empirical fidelity target enters this transform. We divide
each trajectory into 500-step blocks. Candidate starts are
$0,500,\ldots,4000$, and reference starts are $6000,6500,7000,7500$.

\paragraph{Energy gate.}
For trajectory $i$, $D_i(w)$ is the median energy distance
\citep{szekely2013} from candidate block $w$ to its four late blocks. The tolerance is the one-sided
95th percentile of 2,000 null replicates; each resamples calibration trajectories and one of the six
late-block pairs per draw, then takes the replicate median. The checkpoint statistic is
$\operatorname{median}_iD_i(w)$. We define $\wstar$ as the
earliest $w\leq3000$ for which $w,w+500,w+1000$ all fall within tolerance. No such run is reported as
``not verifiably stationary within the horizon,'' not assigned an invented score. On held-out data we
also report whether the same three blocks pass; this transfer check does not refit $\wstar$.

\paragraph{Inference.}
The primary learned-model contrast is the paired held-out Hill difference
$\Delta\alpha=\alpha_{\mathrm{post}}-\alpha_{\mathrm{early}}$. Both estimates use the fixed windows in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fixedlength} and Hill fraction 0.05. The point estimate equally weights checkpoint-level
medians. A 2,000-replicate hierarchical bootstrap first resamples checkpoints and then trajectories.
Eleven other stylized facts and target distances are secondary. Ranking changes are descriptive,
never a pass condition.

\paragraph{Frozen specification and baselines.}
All windows, seeds, thresholds, controls, confidence intervals, and claim tiers were committed before
new learned rollouts or analytic outputs were inspected. Baselines are no discard, fixed $W=500$,
fixed $W=1000$, and a block rule combining ADF and KPSS tests on returns and absolute returns
\citep{dickey1979,kpss1992}. A literature audit after the primary analytic result was frozen added
MSER-5 as an explicitly exploratory comparator; it cannot change the frozen claim tier.

\section{Experiments}

\paragraph{Analytic controls.}
We freeze one previously fitted GARCH-$t$ process and one AR(1) stochastic-volatility process. GARCH
conditions are a 5,000-step long burn, nominal initialization, and initial variance multipliers
$0.1\times$ and $10\times$; AR(1)-SV uses its stationary log-variance law and the two cold multipliers.
Each condition has 1,000 trajectories. The first 992 form 31 pseudo-checkpoints with the same
16/16 split; eight are reserved for file-format checks. Long-burn GARCH and stationary-start AR(1)-SV
are specificity controls; four cold starts measure sensitivity.

\paragraph{Learned checkpoints.}
Seven primary checkpoints cover SPX and BTC baseline/concave-impact pairs plus concave-impact NDX,
gold, and EUR/USD models. Three pre-specified stochastic-volatility checkpoints test within-\method{}
architectural robustness; they are not an independent neural-SDE family. Because the original
checkpoints were unavailable, all ten are retrained from hash-frozen original configurations and data
snapshots on two V100 32GB GPUs, then evaluated with paired explicit seeds. This provenance reset
prevents old aggregate results from being silently paired with new models.

\section{Results}

\paragraph{Specificity passes; sensitivity is narrow.}
Figure~\ref{fig:controls} and Table~\ref{tab:controls} give the pre-specified specificity and cold-start
conditions. The energy gate flags 1/62 stationary pseudo-checkpoints, a 1.61\% false-positive rate
whose Wilson 95\% upper bound is 8.59\%, below the pre-specified 15\% criterion. Cold-start detection is only
28/124 (22.58\%, 95\% CI [16.11, 30.70]). Most power comes from high-variance GARCH initialization;
the gate has little power for the two AR(1)-SV starts or low-variance GARCH. This is evidence for a
conservative audit, not a universal transient detector. The intermediate nominal GARCH start yields
4/31 nonzero warm-up selections (12.90\%).

The ADF/KPSS rule has 50.0\% stationary false positives and is unresolved on 30/31 long-burn GARCH
pseudo-checkpoints. Fixed discards unnecessarily delete data in every stationary case by definition.
The energy gate's pooled median cold-condition Hill error is 0.0507, versus 0.0594 for $W=500$ and
0.0533 for $W=1000$, but its mean error is worse (0.0966 versus 0.0910 and 0.0846). Reporting only the
median would overstate the method advantage.

The post-primary MSER-5 comparison selects a nonzero discard in 16/62 stationary cases (25.81\%) and
76/124 cold starts (61.29\%). Its pooled cold-condition Hill error is 0.0491 by median and 0.0847 by
mean. It is therefore more sensitive than the energy gate but less specific; the energy method's
distinction is conservative selection, not uniformly lower tail-score error.

\paragraph{Learned-model audit.}
Of seven primary checkpoints, \learnedScorable{} yield a calibration $\wstar$ and
\learnedConfirmed{} also pass held-out transfer. Across scorable checkpoints, the equal-weight paired
effect is $\Delta\alpha=\learnedDelta{}$ with hierarchical-bootstrap 95\% interval
\learnedCI{}. The three within-family stochastic-volatility checkpoints have the expected positive
sign in \variantSigns{} cases. Figure~\ref{fig:learned} reports checkpoint-level effects, missing gates, and
all transfer failures; none is removed from the denominator.

\section{Scope, limitations, and conclusion}

This audit separates two questions that a single fidelity score had conflated: what the simulator does
while relaxing, and what it does after a reproducible late-time criterion. The transient is a physical
property of the learned simulator's dynamics, but it is not evidence for real-market physics. This is
distinct from empirical stochastic-thermodynamic analyses of market data \citep{maskawa2025}. Indeed,
a light late-time tail is a model discrepancy when real returns are already heavy tailed. The paper's
value is therefore diagnostic: it withdraws a favorable interpretation instead of rescuing it with a
new scoring convention.

The limits are consequential. Evidence comes from one simulator family, and the analytic sensitivity
is weak except for a strong GARCH variance mismatch. Late blocks provide an operational internal
reference; they do not prove stationarity, mixing, or ergodicity. Energy distance depends on the chosen
delay embedding and finite block length. The Wilson intervals quantify pseudo-checkpoint replication
from disjoint trajectories conditional on two fixed analytic parameterizations, not variation across
process families. Only seven primary checkpoints constrain hierarchical-interval precision. The
learned checkpoints share training code and data sources, and daily financial calibration data plus
one crypto minute dataset do not establish cross-domain generality. Finally, a failed gate says ``not
verified within 8,000 returns,'' not ``nonstationary forever.''

Within those boundaries, the practical recommendation is simple: fit any warm-up decision without
the downstream score or target band, freeze it before held-out evaluation, keep the scored length
fixed, and report both false positives and missed transients. For \method, that protocol determines
whether an apparent heavy-tail success is a late-time model property or initialization contamination.
This can reduce the
risk of drawing scientific or financial conclusions from a simulator artifact; conversely, the
method's missed transients mean that passing it is not a deployment, convergence, or risk-certification
guarantee.

\section{Frozen execution details}

The formal analytic run used 7,000 trajectories of 8,000 returns and a clean committed source tree.
Learned training uses ten 200-iteration configurations at $N=10{,}000$, FP32, and 24-step truncated
BPTT chunks. The exact data, configuration, seed, checkpoint, code, and result hashes are included in
the anonymized artifact. A ten-iteration compatibility probe on each V100 used 14.14 GiB peak allocated
and 15.03 GiB peak reserved memory; the two nodes produced bitwise-identical checkpoint hashes. An
8,000-return inference probe took 388.3 and 383.9 seconds, reserved 3.81 GiB, and produced
bitwise-identical trajectory files.

Daily SPX, NDX, GLD, and EUR/USD observations were accessed through Yahoo Finance; BTC/USDT one-minute
klines came from Binance's public data portal. The artifact includes ingestion code, dates, schemas,
and aggregate hashes, but does not redistribute raw files beyond their source terms.
