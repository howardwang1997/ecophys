\title{Is Simulator Fidelity an Initialization Transient?\\
A Pre-Specified Audit of a Learned Market Model}
\author{Anonymous Author(s)}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Scientific simulators with persistent internal state are often evaluated from fresh, finite rollouts.
If the initialized state distribution is not stationary, a fidelity score can mix startup relaxation
with the regime it purports to measure. We audit this failure mode in a frozen snapshot of \method,
a previously unpublished differentiable Langevin particle market simulator whose return-tail score
motivated a claim of stylized-fact fidelity. We specify that snapshot as the audit object rather than
treating its name as prior validation.
We pre-specify a
split-sample protocol: calibration trajectories set an energy-distance tolerance and the earliest
persistent plateau without using the Hill score or empirical target band; independent trajectories
are then scored on equal-length windows before and after that frozen point. Seven analytic conditions
(7,000 trajectories) yield 1/62
stationary false positives (Wilson 95\% upper bound 8.6\%) but only 28/124 cold-start detections,
showing high specificity and limited sensitivity. Across seven primary learned checkpoints,
\learnedScorable{} are scorable and \learnedConfirmed{} transfer the frozen gate; the paired Hill-index
effect is \learnedDelta{} (95\% interval \learnedCI{}). One primary transfer failure and one unresolved
variant limit the result to a simulator-specific case study. The finding is a simulator discrepancy,
not market physics, and the proposed audit is conservative rather than a universal stationarity test.
It converts an apparently favorable tail score into a falsifiable statement about whether its scored
window is consistent with a frozen late-time reference.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Scientific simulators are imperfect: calibration can reduce parameter error while leaving structural
discrepancy between simulator and reality \citep{kennedy2001}. A more basic discrepancy can enter
\emph{before} calibration is judged. Stateful simulators are commonly launched from convenient
initial conditions, and finite-rollout statistics then mix an initial transient with the intended
long-run regime. Initialization bias is classical in discrete-event simulation
\citep{heidelberger1983,welch1983,white1997}, but it is easy to miss when a learned simulator is
evaluated by a nonlinear distributional summary rather than a sample mean.

We encountered this problem while evaluating \method, a differentiable market simulator with
persistent agent states. Financial return tails provide a sharp test: empirical returns are heavy
tailed \citep{cont2001,gabaix2003,plerou1999}, and a Hill estimate near the inverse-cubic regime is
usually counted as a successful stylized-fact match. In \method, however, the favorable estimate was
concentrated near initialization; later windows were lighter. Thus the same score supported two
opposite readings: realistic steady-state dynamics, or a startup artifact. Existing evidence on
time-varying apparent tails \citep{lebaron2001,warusawitharana2018,quintos2001} makes that distinction
scientifically material.

This paper makes three deliberately narrow contributions. First, it identifies and reproduces the
initialization-contamination failure in a learned Langevin simulator. Second, it pre-specifies a
split-sample audit that chooses a warm-up endpoint without using the Hill score or empirical target
band and preserves the scoring sample length. Third, it stress-tests that audit on analytic
stochastic processes, frozen learned checkpoints, and a within-family stochastic-volatility variant.
The outcome is an audit of an imperfect simulator: we neither infer a real-market mechanism from the
transient nor claim a universal convergence diagnostic.

\section{Audit object and failure mode}

\paragraph{Unpublished audit object.}
\method{} has no prior paper or public release. We use the name only to identify the frozen simulator
snapshot evaluated here; it is neither an external baseline nor an established market model.
Appendix~A gives the complete data flow and specifies the state, networks, price map, initialization,
training objective, and evaluated variants. The audit result does not depend on claiming this
architecture as a contribution.

\paragraph{Learned dynamics.}
The snapshot evolves $N=10{,}000$ agents with latent states $s_i\in\mathbb{R}^{32}$ under overdamped
Langevin dynamics ($\Delta t=0.01$),
\begin{equation}
  s_{i,t+1}=s_{i,t}+\frac{\Delta t}{\gamma_i}
  \left(F^{\mathrm{cons}}_{i,\theta}+F^{\mathrm{diss}}_i\right)
  +\sqrt{\frac{2T_i\Delta t}{\gamma_i}}\,\epsilon_{i,t}+J_{i,t},
\end{equation}
where $F^{\mathrm{cons}}_{i,\theta}=-\nabla_{s_i}U_{\theta}$. A 16-dimensional recurrent global state
conditions a symmetrized random-pair kernel with a learned gate and a per-agent external potential;
$F^{\mathrm{diss}}$ is explicit quadratic drag. The frozen initial law is
$s_{i,0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,0.1^2I)$, with zero global and return memories. Unit-variance
Student-$t_5$ noise, small compound-Poisson inference jumps, and four fixed agent types complete the
state update. Changes in coordinate one form excess demand, followed by either linear or fixed
square-root-like impact and additive price noise. This connects to Langevin market models
\citep{bouchaud1998} and differentiable agent-based calibration \citep{chopra2023,dyer2023}, but the
present contribution is evaluation, not a first-of-kind model claim. Models are trained against
real-data return statistics from two equity indices, gold, EUR/USD, and BTC/USDT.

\paragraph{Contamination.}
Let $S(r_{0:T})$ be a fidelity statistic computed from a freshly initialized rollout. If the initial
law $q_0$ differs from the simulator's stationary or late-time law, $S$ estimates a mixture of
relaxation and late dynamics. Simply deleting $W$ samples creates a second confound when
$S(r_{W:T})$ uses fewer observations. We instead compare
\begin{equation}
 S_0=S(r_{0:L}),\qquad S_W=S(r_{W:W+L}),\quad L=4000,
 \label{eq:fixedlength}
\end{equation}
so that any change is not a sample-length artifact. Our main statistic is the Hill tail index
\citep{hill1975}; larger values indicate lighter tails.

\section{A frozen split-sample audit}

Figure~\ref{fig:protocol} summarizes the protocol. Every checkpoint produces 32 no-shock trajectories
with exactly $T=8000$ usable returns. Sixteen calibration trajectories determine robust scaling, a
tolerance, and $\wstar$; sixteen held-out trajectories are unopened until these quantities are
serialized and hashed.

  Hill score or empirical target band; the earliest three-block plateau gives $\wstar$. It transfers to

  \label{fig:protocol}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Distributional state proxy.}
For each trajectory form delay vectors
$x_t=(r_t,|r_t|,|r_{t+1}|)$. Calibration-late observations ($t\in[6000,8000)$) set a componentwise
median and median absolute deviation; no empirical fidelity target enters this transform. We divide
each trajectory into 500-step blocks. Candidate starts are
$0,500,\ldots,4000$, and reference starts are $6000,6500,7000,7500$.

\paragraph{Energy gate.}
For trajectory $i$, $D_i(w)$ is the median energy distance
\citep{szekely2013} from candidate block $w$ to its four late blocks. The tolerance is the one-sided
95th percentile of 2,000 null replicates; each resamples calibration trajectories and one of the six
late-block pairs per draw, then takes the replicate median. The checkpoint statistic is
$\operatorname{median}_iD_i(w)$. We define $\wstar$ as the
earliest $w\leq3000$ for which $w,w+500,w+1000$ all fall within tolerance. No such run is reported as
``not verifiably stationary within the horizon,'' not assigned an invented score. On held-out data we
also report whether the same three blocks pass; this transfer check does not refit $\wstar$.

\paragraph{Inference.}
The primary learned-model contrast is the paired held-out Hill difference
$\Delta\alpha=\alpha_{\mathrm{post}}-\alpha_{\mathrm{early}}$. Both estimates use the fixed windows in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fixedlength} and Hill fraction 0.05. The point estimate equally weights checkpoint-level
medians. A 2,000-replicate hierarchical bootstrap first resamples checkpoints and then trajectories.
Because rollout seeds are shared across checkpoints, a pre-held-out secondary bootstrap applies one
common seed resample to every selected checkpoint and reports market-balanced leave-one-out effects;
it cannot upgrade the frozen claim tier.
We also freeze Hill tail fractions 0.025, 0.05, and 0.10; only 0.05 is primary.
Eleven other stylized facts and target distances are secondary. Ranking changes are descriptive,
never a pass condition.

\paragraph{Frozen specification and baselines.}
All windows, seeds, thresholds, controls, confidence intervals, and claim tiers were committed before
new learned rollouts or analytic outputs were inspected. Baselines are no discard, fixed $W=500$,
fixed $W=1000$, and a block rule combining ADF and KPSS tests on returns and absolute returns
\citep{dickey1979,kpss1992}. A literature audit after the primary analytic result was frozen added
MSER-5 as an explicitly exploratory comparator; it cannot change the frozen claim tier.

\section{Experiments}

\paragraph{Analytic controls.}
We freeze one previously fitted GARCH-$t$ process and one AR(1) stochastic-volatility process. GARCH
conditions are a 5,000-step long burn, nominal initialization, and initial variance multipliers
$0.1\times$ and $10\times$; AR(1)-SV uses its stationary log-variance law and the two cold multipliers.
Each condition has 1,000 trajectories. The first 992 form 31 pseudo-checkpoints with the same
16/16 split; eight are reserved for file-format checks. Long-burn GARCH and stationary-start AR(1)-SV
are specificity controls; four cold starts measure sensitivity.

\paragraph{Learned checkpoints.}
Seven primary checkpoints cover SPX and BTC baseline/concave-impact pairs plus concave-impact NDX,
gold, and EUR/USD models. Three pre-specified stochastic-volatility checkpoints test within-\method{}
architectural robustness; they are not an independent neural-SDE family. Because the original
checkpoints were unavailable, all ten are retrained from hash-frozen original configurations and data
snapshots on two V100 32GB GPUs, then evaluated with paired explicit seeds. This provenance reset
prevents old aggregate results from being silently paired with new models.

\section{Results}

\paragraph{Specificity passes; sensitivity is narrow.}
Figure~\ref{fig:controls} and Table~\ref{tab:controls} give the pre-specified specificity and cold-start
conditions. The energy gate flags 1/62 stationary pseudo-checkpoints, a 1.61\% false-positive rate
whose Wilson 95\% upper bound is 8.59\%, below the pre-specified 15\% criterion. Cold-start detection is only
28/124 (22.58\%, 95\% CI [16.11, 30.70]). Most power comes from high-variance GARCH initialization;
the gate has little power for the two AR(1)-SV starts or low-variance GARCH. This is evidence for a
conservative audit, not a universal transient detector. The intermediate nominal GARCH start yields
4/31 nonzero warm-up selections (12.90\%).

\paragraph{Learned-model audit.}
Of seven primary checkpoints, \learnedScorable{} yield a calibration $\wstar$ and
\learnedConfirmed{} also pass held-out transfer; the gold checkpoint is the sole transfer failure.
Across scorable checkpoints, the equal-weight paired
effect is $\Delta\alpha=\learnedDelta{}$ with hierarchical-bootstrap 95\% interval
\learnedCI{}. The expected sign occurs in \variantSigns{} within-family variants, but one of the two
scorable variants fails transfer and the third has no $\wstar$; the frozen E3 replication rule therefore
fails. Figure~\ref{fig:learned} reports every missing gate and transfer failure; none is removed from
the denominator.
The common-seed interval is \learnedRobustCI{}, and market-balanced leave-one-out effects span
\learnedLooRange{}. Positive common-seed intervals persist for \hillFractionPasses{} of the frozen
Hill-fraction grid.

\section{Scope, limitations, and conclusion}

This audit separates two questions that a single fidelity score had conflated: what the simulator does
while relaxing, and what it does after a reproducible late-time criterion. The transient is an internal
dynamical property of the learned simulator, but it is not evidence for real-market physics. This is
distinct from empirical stochastic-thermodynamic analyses of market data \citep{maskawa2025}. Indeed,
a light late-time tail is a model discrepancy when real returns are already heavy tailed. The paper's
value is therefore diagnostic: the E-B outcome supports a simulator-specific audit, not broad method
transfer, and withdraws a favorable interpretation instead of rescuing it with a new scoring convention.

The limits are consequential. Evidence comes from one simulator family, and the analytic sensitivity
is weak except for a strong GARCH variance mismatch. Late blocks provide an operational internal
reference; they do not prove stationarity, mixing, or ergodicity. Energy distance depends on the chosen
delay embedding and finite block length. The Wilson intervals quantify pseudo-checkpoint replication
from disjoint trajectories conditional on two fixed analytic parameterizations, not variation across
process families. Learned intervals are conditional on one frozen calibration split and omit gate-
selection uncertainty. Only seven primary checkpoints constrain hierarchical-interval precision, and
most asset/configuration cells have one training seed. The suite was chosen from prior model-
development runs; its checkpoints share training code and data sources. Daily financial calibration
data plus one crypto minute dataset do not establish cross-domain generality. Finally, a failed gate
says ``not
verified within 8,000 returns,'' not ``nonstationary forever.''

Within those boundaries, the practical recommendation is simple: fit any warm-up decision without
the downstream score or target band, freeze it before held-out evaluation, keep the scored length
fixed, and report both false positives and missed transients. For \method, the protocol tests whether
an apparent heavy-tail success survives a frozen late-time criterion; it does not certify stationarity.
This can reduce the
risk of drawing scientific or financial conclusions from a simulator artifact; conversely, the
method's missed transients mean that passing it is not a deployment, convergence, or risk-certification
guarantee.

\label{app:ecomd}

This appendix defines the previously unpublished simulator snapshot used to generate the learned
trajectories. ``v0-audit'' is a version label for this paper, not a claim that the broader research
codebase is a stable public API.

\subsection{State, initial law, and Langevin update}

There are $N=10{,}000$ agents, each with $s_{i,t}\in\mathbb{R}^{32}$; coordinate one is interpreted
as a signed position only by the price map. At rollout start,
\begin{equation}
s_{i,0}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,0.1^2I_{32}),\quad
s_{i,-1}=s_{i,0},\quad u_0=0,\quad
(p_0,r_{-1},v_0,h_0)=(0,0,0.005,0).
\end{equation}
Four type labels are drawn once from a seeded uniform categorical law and then held fixed. With base
friction $\gamma=10$ and temperature $T=0.05$, their respective multipliers are
$(0.7,1.5,1.0,0.5)$ and $(0.5,2.0,1.0,0.3)$ (type seed 42). The Euler--Maruyama update in Section~2
uses $\Delta t=0.01$ and iid Student-$t_5$ innovations rescaled to unit variance.

At inference, each component of $J_{i,t}$ is
$0.01\sqrt{K_{i,t}}Z_{i,t}$, where $K_{i,t}\sim\mathrm{Poisson}(0.5\Delta t)$ and
$Z_{i,t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$. During gradient-based training, the discrete jump is replaced by the
deterministic differentiable drift proxy
$-(0.5)(0.01)\Delta t\tanh(s_{i,t})$. This training--inference mismatch is part of the audited
implementation; the audit does not interpret it as market physics.

\subsection{Learned potential and global state}

Let $\mathcal{E}_{i,t}$ contain $k=50$ distinct partners sampled uniformly for agent $i$ at every
step. Define $z_{ij,t}=[s_{i,t},s_{j,t},|s_{i,t}-s_{j,t}|,u_t]$ and
\begin{align}
\bar\phi_\theta(z_{ij,t})
  &=\tfrac12\{\phi_\theta(\operatorname{LN}z_{ij,t})
                 +\phi_\theta(\operatorname{LN}z_{ji,t})\},\\
U_{\mathrm{pair},\theta}
  &=\frac{N-1}{2k}\sum_i\sum_{j\in\mathcal{E}_{i,t}}
    \frac{\sigma(G_\theta(\operatorname{LN}z_{ij,t}))}{0.7}
    \bar\phi_\theta(z_{ij,t}),\\
U_{\mathrm{ext},\theta}
  &=\sum_i\psi_\theta([s_{i,t},p_t,v_t,r_{t-1},u_t]),\qquad
U_\theta=U_{\mathrm{pair},\theta}+U_{\mathrm{ext},\theta}.
\end{align}
The prefactor makes the ordered random-edge sum an unbiased estimator of the corresponding full-pair
sum; the kernel is explicitly symmetrized, while the directed gate is averaged only in expectation
over ordered sampling. Both $\phi_\theta$ and $\psi_\theta$ are two-hidden-layer MLPs with widths $(96,96)$ and SiLU
activations; $G_\theta$ has one width-96 SiLU hidden layer. The gate is initialized to probability
0.7 and divided by 0.7 to preserve the initial potential scale. The explicit dissipative potential is
\begin{equation}
U_{\mathrm{diss}}=0.01\sum_i\lVert s_{i,t}-s_{i,t-1}\rVert_2^2,\qquad
F^{\mathrm{diss}}_{i,t}=-\nabla_{s_{i,t}}U_{\mathrm{diss}}.
\end{equation}

Before forces are evaluated, a learned projection $P:\mathbb{R}^{32}\to\mathbb{R}^{8}$ and a
GRUCell update the global state at every step,
\begin{equation}
u_t=\operatorname{GRUCell}\!\left(
 [P(N^{-1}\textstyle\sum_i s_{i,t}),v_t,|r_{t-1}|,r_{t-1}],u_{t-1}\right),
 \qquad u_t\in\mathbb{R}^{16}.
\end{equation}
The same $u_t$ enters both potential terms. No MACE, geometric equivariance, learned agent memory,
or learned regime-switching module is active in these checkpoints.

\subsection{Price formation}

For $\Delta s_{i,t}=s_{i,t+1}-s_{i,t}$, raw excess demand and the two frozen impact maps are
\begin{equation}
q_t=0.5\sum_i\Delta s^{(1)}_{i,t},\qquad
I_{\mathrm{lin}}(q)=q,\qquad
I_{1/2}(q)=\operatorname{sign}(q)\,0.5
 \left(\frac{|q|}{0.5}+10^{-8}\right)^{1/2}.
\end{equation}
The concave map is motivated only as a frozen price-impact variant
\citep{toth2011}; this paper does not test an empirical impact law. With $\xi_t\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$,
\begin{align}
r_t^{\mathrm{core}}&=0.02I(q_t)-\tfrac12(0.005)^2+0.005\xi_t,\\
r_t&=r_t^{\mathrm{core}}+0.3h_t\operatorname{sign}(r_t^{\mathrm{core}}),
&p_{t+1}&=p_t+r_t,\\
h_{t+1}&=0.9h_t+0.1|r_t^{\mathrm{core}}|,
&v_{t+1}&=0.95v_t+0.05|r_t|.
\end{align}
The context $(p_{t+1},v_{t+1},r_t)$ is fed back at the next simulator step. Reported volume is
$\sum_i|\Delta s^{(1)}_{i,t}|$.

\subsection{Training objective and evaluated variants}

All checkpoints use FP32 Adam for 200 outer iterations, learning rate $10^{-3}$ with a ten-iteration
linear warm-up, gradient-norm clipping at 100, 24-step truncated-BPTT chunks, and 16 discarded
startup steps per chunk. Agent and price states persist across outer iterations but are detached at
every chunk boundary. In the frozen training implementation, $u$ is reinitialized to zero at each
24-step chunk because it is not returned by the chunk API; in 8,000-step inference it persists for
the whole rollout. Every fourth iteration (every eighth for the stochastic-volatility variant), a
fresh 512-step rollout adds a long-horizon regularizer, with state gradients detached and $u$ reset
every 24 steps. This is a second disclosed training--inference mismatch.

The structural loss is an L1-weighted combination of squared-return ACF mismatch (weight 1.0, lags
1--8), leverage mismatch (0.2), a differentiable Hill mismatch at tail fraction 0.05 (0.1), raw-return
lag-one autocorrelation magnitude (0.5), and a hinge penalty above Hill index 10 (0.3). A
multi-bandwidth Gaussian MMD to the real return sample has weight 50 and is evaluated only on the
512-step long rollouts; the short-chunk call has no target-return array and therefore contains only
the structural terms. Long-rollout regularizer weight is 1.0. These details matter because the
audited 8,000-step inference horizon is much longer than either training horizon.

Seven primary checkpoints comprise SPX and BTC linear/concave pairs plus concave NDX, gold, and
EUR/USD models. Daily targets for SPX, NDX, gold, and EUR/USD cover 2015--2026; BTC/USDT uses 2024-Q1
one-minute returns. Three SPX checkpoints add a three-dimensional learned stochastic-volatility
latent to both the price-noise scale and Langevin-noise scale. They retain the same potential and are
therefore a within-family variant, not an independent neural-SDE simulator. For component $a$,
\begin{align}
m_{a,t}&=(1-\kappa_a)v_{a,t}
+\kappa_a[\mu_a+w^{|r|}_a|r_t|+w^-_a\max(-r_t,0)]+\xi_a z_{a,t},\\
v_{a,t+1}&=3\tanh(m_{a,t}/3),\qquad
\bar v_t=\sum_a\operatorname{softmax}(\theta)_a v_{a,t}.
\end{align}
Here $z_{a,t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)$; all displayed coefficients and mixing logits are learned.
Initial values are $\kappa=(0.5,0.1,0.02)$, $\mu=0$, $w^{|r|}=w^-=0.1$, $\xi=0.1$, and equal
mixing weights. Price noise is multiplied by $\exp(g\bar v_{t+1})$ with learned $g$ initialized at
0.5, while the agent-state noise at step $t$ is multiplied by $\exp(\bar v_t)$. Configuration files
in the supplement are the binding machine-readable specification.

\section{Execution and reproduction boundary}

The formal analytic run used 7,000 trajectories of 8,000 returns and a clean committed source tree.
Vectorized trajectory generation took 82.7 CPU-seconds and the frozen gate/baseline analysis took
564.3 CPU-seconds on the local execution environment.
Learned training uses ten 200-iteration configurations at $N=10{,}000$, FP32, and 24-step truncated
BPTT chunks. The supplement records exact data, configuration, seed, checkpoint, code, trajectory,
and result hashes. A ten-iteration compatibility probe on each V100 used 14.14 GiB peak allocated
and 15.03 GiB peak reserved memory; the two nodes produced bitwise-identical checkpoint hashes. An
8,000-return inference probe took 388.3 and 383.9 seconds, reserved 3.81 GiB, and produced
bitwise-identical trajectory files. The ten training runs consumed 19.90 V100 GPU-hours and the 320
formal learned rollouts consumed 34.51, for 54.40 V100 GPU-hours in total.

Daily SPX, NDX, GLD, and EUR/USD observations were accessed through Yahoo Finance; BTC/USDT one-minute
klines came from Binance's public data portal. Dates, schemas, provenance, and aggregate hashes are
included, but raw files are not redistributed beyond their source terms.

The anonymous review artifact is deliberately an \emph{audit artifact}: it contains the frozen
synthetic trajectories, gate and result records, analytic controls, estimators, analysis and figure
code, exact configurations, and tests needed to recompute every number in this paper. It excludes
the broader simulator/training implementation and learned checkpoint binaries, so it does not
support checkpoint retraining or trajectory regeneration. The full EcoMD method and software require
a separate model paper and release after stationary-fidelity defects are addressed. This boundary
prevents the present evaluation paper from silently functioning as the model's first software
release, while the equations above keep the object under review scientifically inspectable.
